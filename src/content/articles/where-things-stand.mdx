Step back from the blur of version numbers and a few things are clear.

In under a decade, AI has gone from one 2017 research paper to a technology hundreds of millions
of people use daily and governments treat as national strategy. The contest has settled into a
clear shape. On the American side sit OpenAI (tied closely to Microsoft), Anthropic, Google, Meta
and Elon Musk's xAI on X. On the Chinese side sits a broad, fast-moving field led by DeepSeek,
Alibaba, Moonshot, ByteDance and others, set apart by how far it has leaned into open source
<Ref n={25} />. The rivalry between the two countries, over chips, over talent, over whether AI
should be locked away or handed out freely, is now one of the defining contests of the era.

The technology has moved through clear stages: from systems that could only read and write, to
ones that could see and speak, to ones that pause to reason, to ones that increasingly act for us
as agents. Each stage arrived faster than the one before.

The biggest questions are still open. Whether the hundreds of billions going into data centres pay
off or pop. Whether open or closed development wins out. Whether these more capable, more
autonomous systems can be kept safe and pointed at what people actually want, which was the worry
that led to Anthropic's founding back in 2021, and one that gets sharper as the machines get more
powerful. The history of modern AI is, so far, a story with real momentum and no clear ending. If
the last nine years are any guide, the next chapter will land sooner than we expect.
