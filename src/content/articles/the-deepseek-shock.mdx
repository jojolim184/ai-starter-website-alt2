If one moment defined 2025, it landed in the last week of January. DeepSeek, the Chinese startup,
released a reasoning model called R1, and gave it away free, as open source <Ref n={19} />. What
stunned the industry wasn't only that it held its own against the best American systems. It was
the price: DeepSeek said it had built the model for a tiny fraction of what its US rivals were
spending, and did it despite American export controls meant to keep China away from the most
advanced chips <Ref n={19} />.

The reaction was sharp. American tech stocks tumbled, and the chipmaker Nvidia, whose processors
run most AI systems, had hundreds of billions of dollars wiped off its value, with one widely
cited figure putting the drop near $593 billion <Ref n={20} />. The US market as a whole shed
something like a trillion dollars, and even President Trump called it a wake-up call <Ref n={19} />.
DeepSeek briefly became the most downloaded free app in the United States <Ref n={18} />. The whole
episode punctured a comfortable assumption: that cutting-edge AI would stay the preserve of a few
rich American companies. World-class AI could be built cheaply and handed out openly, and China had
just shown it was a real peer in the race, not a distant follower.

The American response ran on two fronts. On the first, the labs kept shipping better systems at a
punishing rate. In February, Anthropic released a Claude model that could switch between quick
answers and careful reasoning, paired with a well-liked developer tool called Claude Code, and
followed with its Claude 4 generation in May <Ref n={16} />. OpenAI, after plenty of build-up,
released GPT-5 in August, a system built to decide for itself when a question needed a fast reply
and when it needed slower, careful thought <Ref n={21} />. Google, Anthropic, xAI and OpenAI all
pushed out major upgrades through the year, often leapfrogging one another within days.

The second front was money, and staggering amounts of it. In January, alongside the incoming Trump
administration, OpenAI, Japan's SoftBank and Oracle announced Stargate, a plan to put up to $500
billion into American AI infrastructure: vast data centres packed with chips, drawing enough
electricity to run cities <Ref n={22} />. Nvidia agreed to invest as much as $100 billion in
OpenAI, and other giants committed hundreds of billions more <Ref n={22} />. The numbers were big
enough to unsettle even the optimists, and talk of an "AI bubble" got louder: a worry that the
industry was spending far faster than it was earning, inside a web of deals where the same
companies invested in each other and bought each other's products <Ref n={23} />.

Two other themes settled in during 2025. The first was agents: AI that doesn't just answer
questions but does things for you, like browsing the web, filling in forms, or running a
multi-step task from start to finish <Ref n={24} />. The second was the deepening US-China
rivalry. China leaned into open source on purpose. Beyond DeepSeek and Alibaba's Qwen, the likes
of Moonshot AI (with its Kimi models), ByteDance, Zhipu and Baidu put out capable systems, many of
them free to download <Ref n={25} />. Where American leaders such as OpenAI mostly kept their best
work behind closed doors, Chinese firms bet that giving theirs away would buy them global
influence and developer loyalty, and help them work around American chip restrictions <Ref n={26} />.
