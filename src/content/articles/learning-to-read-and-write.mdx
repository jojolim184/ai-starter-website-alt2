Two companies worked out quickly what the transformer could do.

Google built a system called BERT in 2018 and used it to make its search engine far better at
working out what people actually meant when they typed a query <Ref n={7} />. This was AI doing
its work quietly, out of sight.

A then-small research lab called OpenAI, founded in 2015 with backing from figures including Elon
Musk and Sam Altman, took a different path. It built a run of systems it called GPT, short for
"generative pre-trained transformer". The idea was simple: feed the thing a vast amount of text
from the internet and train it to do one job, predict the next word <Ref n={8} />. Do that well
enough, at a big enough scale, and the writing that comes out starts to read as fluent and
coherent.

GPT-1 in 2018 was a modest proof of concept. GPT-2, in 2019, was good enough that OpenAI held back
the full version at first, worried it could be used to churn out fake news and spam at scale
<Ref n={8} />. The caution drew attention, and it flagged a tension that has trailed AI ever since:
the same tools that help can also do harm.
