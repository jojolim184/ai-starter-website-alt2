If 2022 lit the fuse, 2023 was the bang. Every big technology company scrambled to respond, and
the shape of the modern industry set in.

OpenAI raised the bar in March with GPT-4, a much more capable system that could take images as
well as text and did well on everything from bar exams to coding tests <Ref n={8} />. With a
multibillion-dollar partnership behind it from Microsoft, which built the technology into its own
products, OpenAI was the company to beat.

Google, which had invented the transformer but moved slower on consumer products, found itself on
the back foot. It rushed out a chatbot called Bard, then later in the year put forward a stronger
family of models called Gemini <Ref n={13} />. The company that quietly pioneered the underlying
science was now playing catch-up in public.

Anthropic launched its assistant, Claude, in March 2023, pitched as a more careful, steerable
option with safety front and centre <Ref n={14} />. Big investment followed, including from Amazon
and Google.

Meta (Facebook's parent company) took a different path, and it mattered. In February 2023 it
released its LLaMA models as open source, meaning anyone could download and build on them for
free, which kicked off a wider open-weights movement <Ref n={13} />. That "open" approach, set
against the "closed" one favoured by OpenAI and Anthropic, became one of the defining fault lines
in AI, and it's the one where Chinese companies would later plant their flag.

Finally, Elon Musk, who had co-founded OpenAI years earlier before walking away, started a new
venture, xAI, in July, tying it closely to his social network, by then renamed X (formerly
Twitter) <Ref n={15} />. By November, xAI had its own chatbot, Grok, sold as cheekier and less
filtered than its rivals, with a direct line into the firehose of posts on X <Ref n={13} />. The
main American line-up, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta and xAI, was now basically set.
