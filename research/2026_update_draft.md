## 2026: Export controls, a trillion-dollar race to list, and the pace that wouldn't slow

The rhythm of late 2025 carried straight into the new year. Anthropic shipped Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6 in February, Opus 4.7 in April and Opus 4.8 in May, while OpenAI, Google and xAI matched it release for release [29]. Google used its I/O conference in May to announce the Gemini 3.5 line, starting with a fast Flash model and promising a Pro version to follow [43].

China kept pace in the open. In April, DeepSeek released V4 as open source in two sizes, and adapted it to run on Huawei's Ascend chips, so that a leading Chinese model no longer depended on Nvidia hardware. Within days, ByteDance, Tencent and Alibaba were reported to be chasing Huawei for new chip orders [38]. In July, Moonshot followed with Kimi K3, billed as the largest open-source model in the world [30].

### The week a government switched off a model

The most unusual event of the year came in June. On 9 June Anthropic released Claude Fable 5 to the public, alongside Mythos 5, a still more capable model [33]. Three days later the US Commerce Department ordered Anthropic to cut off access to both models for every foreign national, a directive that forced the company to take them offline for everyone [33], [34]. The standoff lasted about two weeks. On 30 June Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick announced the controls would be lifted, after Anthropic agreed to detect security risks and coordinate with the government on protocols for these and future models, and access began returning on 1 July [34]. The episode showed how quickly a chatbot company's product had become a matter of national security.

### A new generation, and a new complaint

September brought the next round of flagships. On 3 September OpenAI began a phased release of GPT-6 Astra, first to approved business testers under tighter cybersecurity guardrails, and described it as meaningfully approximating artificial general intelligence [35]. That is OpenAI's own characterisation, and it remains contested. Anthropic answered on 22 September with Claude Opus 5.5, which it priced about 40 per cent below Opus 5 and which handles a million tokens of context [36]. By early September the sheer cadence had a name of its own. CNBC reported "model fatigue" setting in, as buyers struggled to keep up with new versions arriving every few weeks [37].

### The money, and the listings

The capital behind all this grew to match. In late May Anthropic raised US$65 billion at a US$965 billion valuation, putting it ahead of OpenAI's US$852 billion for the first time, and reported annualised revenue of about US$47 billion [39]. OpenAI had confidentially filed paperwork for a public listing on 22 May and Anthropic did the same on 1 June [39], [40]. Both are reported to be aiming for the final months of 2026, and either would rank among the largest share offerings on record. As of early October, neither has yet listed.

The chip makers are feeling it. Nvidia reported US$96.2 billion in quarterly revenue in its latest results, more than double the same quarter a year earlier [41]. The bubble argument hasn't gone away, because critics still point to the distance between infrastructure spending and the revenue that has so far come back. Those figures keep rising, and nobody can yet say whether the returns will follow.

### Rules, patchily

Regulation moved far more slowly than the models. The European Union's AI Act began applying its obligations for high-risk systems in August 2026 [42]. The United States still has no federal AI law, and a federal push to override state rules has met resistance from states such as California, New York and Colorado [42]. Australia has no AI-specific legislation either. It applies existing privacy, consumer and discrimination law to AI and relies on voluntary guidance [42].
