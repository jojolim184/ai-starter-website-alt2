Important History
Alan Turing
Long before anyone used the phrase artificial intelligence, Alan Turing was already turning over the idea of a thinking machine. Turing was the mathematician who cracked Enigma, the German cipher machine used in the Second World War [1]. After the war he helped design some of the earliest computers, laying groundwork that still sits under the technology we use today [1]. In 1950 he took on the harder question of whether a machine could think, and put forward the Turing Test as a way of deciding whether one was really "intelligent" [2].
The phrase itself arrived soon after. It was coined in the 1955 proposal for the Dartmouth Workshop, which ran the following year and is usually treated as the birth of the field [3]. The two decades from 1956 to 1976 brought real progress, most notably the perceptron, an early ancestor of the neural networks that everything now rests on [4].
AI Winter, Resurgence and BOOM
Then it stalled. Funding dried up and the criticism piled on, and the stretch from 1974 to 1980 became known as the "AI Winter" [5]. AI only really came back into public view with Deep Blue, the IBM chess machine that famously beat grandmaster Garry Kasparov [5].
From there the pace picked up. Between 2005 and 2017 the field sped along, helped by two things arriving at once: an ocean of data thrown off by everything we did on computers and the internet, and hardware that kept getting cheaper and more capable. That window produced AlexNet, the rise of deep neural networks, and AlphaGo [6].
Arguably the biggest shift of the lot is the transformer, introduced in the now-famous paper "Attention Is All You Need" [7]. It rewrote how these networks were built, and it became the backbone of the large language models (LLMs) we all use today [7].

The Modern Landscape
A roughly 15-minute read
2018–2019: Learning to read and write
Two companies worked out quickly what the transformer could do.
Google built a system called BERT in 2018 and used it to make its search engine far better at working out what people actually meant when they typed a query [7]. This was AI doing its work quietly, out of sight.
A then-small research lab called OpenAI, founded in 2015 with backing from figures including Elon Musk and Sam Altman, took a different path. It built a run of systems it called GPT, short for "generative pre-trained transformer". The idea was simple: feed the thing a vast amount of text from the internet and train it to do one job, predict the next word [8]. Do that well enough, at a big enough scale, and the writing that comes out starts to read as fluent and coherent.
GPT-1 in 2018 was a modest proof of concept. GPT-2, in 2019, was good enough that OpenAI held back the full version at first, worried it could be used to churn out fake news and spam at scale [8]. The caution drew attention, and it flagged a tension that has trailed AI ever since: the same tools that help can also do harm.
2020: Bigger is better
In 2020, OpenAI released GPT-3, and it shifted the whole conversation. It was more than a hundred times the size of its predecessor, trained on a large slice of the public internet [8]. The lesson developers took away was plain: make these systems bigger, feed them more data, and they get noticeably smarter, often in ways nobody predicted. GPT-3 could write essays, answer questions, draft emails and knock out simple code, none of which it had been trained to do specifically.
This "scaling" insight, the idea that raw size and data could stand in for clever hand-built design, became the industry's guiding bet for the next few years. It also meant AI was about to get expensive, because bigger systems need more chips, more electricity and more money. GPT-3 mostly stayed with developers and businesses, so the wider public hadn't felt the change yet.
2021: A new lab, and machines that make pictures
Two developments stand out from 2021.
First, a group of senior OpenAI staff, led by siblings Dario and Daniela Amodei, left to start their own company, Anthropic, set up that year with a focus on AI safety [9]. Their worry was that AI was moving faster than anyone's ability to make it safe and predictable, so they wanted a lab where safety research and frontier development sat side by side. Anthropic would go on to build the Claude family of AI assistants and a training method it called "constitutional AI" [9]. The split said something about the field too: arguments over how fast to move, and how much caution to apply, would keep reshaping who worked where.
Second, OpenAI showed transformers weren't only for words. Its DALL-E system, announced in January 2021, could take a written description, say "an armchair shaped like an avocado", and produce an original image to match [10]. The machines were starting to create, not just read and write.
2022: The year everything went public
For five years this had mostly played out away from the public. In 2022 it broke into the open.
The first wave was visual. Stable Diffusion and Midjourney, both of which reached the public in 2022, let anyone type a sentence and get back a striking image seconds later [11]. Social media filled up with AI art almost overnight, and artists and photographers started warning that their work was being copied and their livelihoods put at risk.
Then, on 30 November 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT [12]. Underneath, it was a friendly chat window sitting on top of the GPT technology the company had spent years refining. The plain conversational wrapper is what changed everything. Ordinary people could now ask an AI to explain an idea, write a poem, plan a trip or fix their code, and get back an answer that felt unnervingly human. ChatGPT passed an estimated hundred million users in about two months, one of the fastest take-ups of any product on record [12]. This was the moment "AI" entered the everyday vocabulary of hundreds of millions of people. The race was public now, and it was on.
2023: The gold rush
If 2022 lit the fuse, 2023 was the bang. Every big technology company scrambled to respond, and the shape of the modern industry set in.
OpenAI raised the bar in March with GPT-4, a much more capable system that could take images as well as text and did well on everything from bar exams to coding tests [8]. With a multibillion-dollar partnership behind it from Microsoft, which built the technology into its own products, OpenAI was the company to beat.
Google, which had invented the transformer but moved slower on consumer products, found itself on the back foot. It rushed out a chatbot called Bard, then later in the year put forward a stronger family of models called Gemini [13]. The company that quietly pioneered the underlying science was now playing catch-up in public.
Anthropic launched its assistant, Claude, in March 2023, pitched as a more careful, steerable option with safety front and centre [14]. Big investment followed, including from Amazon and Google.
Meta (Facebook's parent company) took a different path, and it mattered. In February 2023 it released its LLaMA models as open source, meaning anyone could download and build on them for free, which kicked off a wider open-weights movement [13]. That "open" approach, set against the "closed" one favoured by OpenAI and Anthropic, became one of the defining fault lines in AI, and it's the one where Chinese companies would later plant their flag.
Finally, Elon Musk, who had co-founded OpenAI years earlier before walking away, started a new venture, xAI, in July, tying it closely to his social network, by then renamed X (formerly Twitter) [15]. By November, xAI had its own chatbot, Grok, sold as cheekier and less filtered than its rivals, with a direct line into the firehose of posts on X [13]. The main American line-up, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta and xAI, was now basically set.
2024: Talking, seeing, and starting to think
With the big players in place, 2024 was about making the systems faster, stronger and more natural to use, and about one new capability that changed the game.
In the northern spring, OpenAI released GPT-4o, which could see, hear and speak close to real time, holding a spoken conversation with barely a pause [8]. Around the same time Anthropic shipped its Claude 3 family, then an upgraded Claude 3.5 that developers took to for its coding [14], [16]. Google's Gemini models learned to handle enormous inputs in one go, whole books or hours of video at a time. Late in the year Anthropic gave Claude an early knack for operating a computer directly, moving a cursor and clicking, an early look at where things were headed [16].
The bigger shift was quieter. In September, OpenAI introduced a system nicknamed o1, built on a simple idea: rather than blurting out the first answer it landed on, the model would take extra time to work through a problem step by step, the way a person scribbles on scratch paper before answering something hard [8]. These became known as reasoning models, and they were much better at maths, science and logic puzzles. Nearly every major lab picked up the approach soon after. Bigger was no longer the only path to smarter. Thinking for longer was now on the menu as well.
2024 was also when the Chinese players moved from the margins toward the centre. Alibaba's Qwen models became some of the most downloaded open-source systems in the world, so common that when a developer anywhere fine-tuned "an open model", it was very often a Qwen underneath [17]. And a little-known startup founded in 2023, DeepSeek, put out a strong model late in the year that caught the experts' eye [18]. Few outside the field saw what was coming.
2025: The DeepSeek shock, the money, and the flood
If one moment defined 2025, it landed in the last week of January. DeepSeek, the Chinese startup, released a reasoning model called R1, and gave it away free, as open source [19]. What stunned the industry wasn't only that it held its own against the best American systems. It was the price: DeepSeek said it had built the model for a tiny fraction of what its US rivals were spending, and did it despite American export controls meant to keep China away from the most advanced chips [19].
The reaction was sharp. American tech stocks tumbled, and the chipmaker Nvidia, whose processors run most AI systems, had hundreds of billions of dollars wiped off its value, with one widely cited figure putting the drop near $593 billion [20]. The US market as a whole shed something like a trillion dollars, and even President Trump called it a wake-up call [19]. DeepSeek briefly became the most downloaded free app in the United States [18]. The whole episode punctured a comfortable assumption: that cutting-edge AI would stay the preserve of a few rich American companies. World-class AI could be built cheaply and handed out openly, and China had just shown it was a real peer in the race, not a distant follower.
The American response ran on two fronts. On the first, the labs kept shipping better systems at a punishing rate. In February, Anthropic released a Claude model that could switch between quick answers and careful reasoning, paired with a well-liked developer tool called Claude Code, and followed with its Claude 4 generation in May [16]. OpenAI, after plenty of build-up, released GPT-5 in August, a system built to decide for itself when a question needed a fast reply and when it needed slower, careful thought [21]. Google, Anthropic, xAI and OpenAI all pushed out major upgrades through the year, often leapfrogging one another within days.
The second front was money, and staggering amounts of it. In January, alongside the incoming Trump administration, OpenAI, Japan's SoftBank and Oracle announced Stargate, a plan to put up to $500 billion into American AI infrastructure: vast data centres packed with chips, drawing enough electricity to run cities [22]. Nvidia agreed to invest as much as $100 billion in OpenAI, and other giants committed hundreds of billions more [22]. The numbers were big enough to unsettle even the optimists, and talk of an "AI bubble" got louder: a worry that the industry was spending far faster than it was earning, inside a web of deals where the same companies invested in each other and bought each other's products [23].
Two other themes settled in during 2025. The first was agents: AI that doesn't just answer questions but does things for you, like browsing the web, filling in forms, or running a multi-step task from start to finish [24]. The second was the deepening US-China rivalry. China leaned into open source on purpose. Beyond DeepSeek and Alibaba's Qwen, the likes of Moonshot AI (with its Kimi models), ByteDance, Zhipu and Baidu put out capable systems, many of them free to download [25]. Where American leaders such as OpenAI mostly kept their best work behind closed doors, Chinese firms bet that giving theirs away would buy them global influence and developer loyalty, and help them work around American chip restrictions [26].
Late 2025 into 2026: AI’s "Space Race"
The pace, already frantic, tipped into something almost comical late in 2025. Across about three weeks in November and December, four of the biggest labs each put out their most powerful system yet: xAI's Grok 4.1, then Google's Gemini 3 a day later, then Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.5, then OpenAI's GPT-5.2 [27]. Google pushed Gemini 3 to billions of search users on day one [28]. Each launch was upstaged by the next before anyone had time to take it in. Commentators, only half-joking, called the tempo "singularity speed", the point where progress compounds faster than anyone can keep track of [27].
That rhythm carried straight into 2026. Anthropic in particular shipped update after update, Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6 in February, Opus 4.7 in April and Opus 4.8 in May, while OpenAI, Google and xAI matched it release for release [29]. Anthropic also brought in a new top tier of especially capable models under the "Mythos" name, at first only for a small set of trusted organisations doing safety-sensitive work [29]. By the middle of 2026 the upshot is a field where the "best model" changes month to month and depends heavily on what you're trying to do.
China's momentum hasn't let up either. By 2026 its AI scene is no longer a one-company story. DeepSeek, Alibaba's Qwen, Moonshot's Kimi, ByteDance's Doubao, Zhipu's GLM and Baidu's ERNIE all compete on different strengths, most of them shipping open-weight models that match American systems on price [25]. In July 2026 Moonshot released Kimi K3 and billed it as the largest open-source model in the world, a sign of how firmly China now leads on open, freely available AI, even while some markets stay wary about trusting models built in China [30].
Underneath the model races, the money kept swelling. The five largest American cloud and AI infrastructure companies, Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta and Oracle, together lined up somewhere between $660 and $690 billion in spending for 2026 alone, close to double the year before [31]. Stargate's first data centres came online in Texas and broke ground in Michigan and beyond [22], [32]. The bubble argument sharpened along with it. Sceptics point to circular financing and enormous costs that revenue hasn't justified yet, while believers say the productivity gains are real and still only starting [23]. As of the middle of 2026 the bubble hasn't burst, though the question of whether it will hangs over the whole industry.
Where things stand today
Step back from the blur of version numbers and a few things are clear.
In under a decade, AI has gone from one 2017 research paper to a technology hundreds of millions of people use daily and governments treat as national strategy. The contest has settled into a clear shape. On the American side sit OpenAI (tied closely to Microsoft), Anthropic, Google, Meta and Elon Musk's xAI on X. On the Chinese side sits a broad, fast-moving field led by DeepSeek, Alibaba, Moonshot, ByteDance and others, set apart by how far it has leaned into open source [25]. The rivalry between the two countries, over chips, over talent, over whether AI should be locked away or handed out freely, is now one of the defining contests of the era.
The technology has moved through clear stages: from systems that could only read and write, to ones that could see and speak, to ones that pause to reason, to ones that increasingly act for us as agents. Each stage arrived faster than the one before.
The biggest questions are still open. Whether the hundreds of billions going into data centres pay off or pop. Whether open or closed development wins out. Whether these more capable, more autonomous systems can be kept safe and pointed at what people actually want, which was the worry that led to Anthropic's founding back in 2021, and one that gets sharper as the machines get more powerful. The history of modern AI is, so far, a story with real momentum and no clear ending. If the last nine years are any guide, the next chapter will land sooner than we expect.
